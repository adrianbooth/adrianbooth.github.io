How the shell rules work

1. Atomic number = electron countIn a neutral atom, the atomic number gives the number of protons and also the number of electrons.
2. Electrons fill lower-energy states firstElectrons occupy available lower-energy orbitals before higher-energy ones. Shells are energy levels, each containing subshells.
3. Shell capacityA shell with principal level n can hold up to 2n² electrons in total. For example, shell 1 holds 2 and shell 2 holds 8. This is a maximum capacity, not always the order shells fill.
4. Why simple rules have exceptionsSubshells from different shells can be close in energy. Their order can shift, and half-filled or filled subshells can be especially stable. So transition metals and heavier elements do not follow a simple “fill each shell to capacity” pattern.

The displayed arrangements use commonly taught ground-state electron configurations converted into shell totals. A few very heavy elements have uncertain or debated configurations, so their shell totals should be treated as approximate.

Extra learning: why shells do not simply fill to 2n²

The key idea: 2n² tells you the maximum capacity of a shell, not the order electrons fill in. Electrons fill the available orbitals with the lowest energy first. Some orbitals in a higher shell can be lower in energy than orbitals in a lower shell, so the filling order crosses between shells.

1. Calculate a shell's maximum capacity Use 2n², where n is the shell number.
  • Shell 1: 2 × 1² = 2
  • Shell 2: 2 × 2² = 8
  • Shell 3: 2 × 3² = 18
  • Shell 4: 2 × 4² = 32
  • Shell 5: 2 × 5² = 50
  • Shell 6: 2 × 6² = 72

So shells can have a capacity greater than 32. The number 32 is the maximum for shell 4 only.

2. What is a subshell? Each shell is split into subshells called s, p, d and f. They have different capacities:
  • s: 2 electrons
  • p: 6 electrons
  • d: 10 electrons
  • f: 14 electrons

For example, shell 3 contains 3s, 3p and 3d, so its total capacity is 2 + 6 + 10 = 18.

3. Why does shell 4 start before shell 3 is full? The 4s subshell is usually lower in energy than 3d when these begin filling, so potassium and calcium place electrons in 4s first. After that, electrons begin filling 3d.

That is why potassium is 2, 8, 8, 1 rather than 2, 8, 9. Shell 3 has room for 18 in total, but it does not have to fill to 18 before shell 4 starts.

4. Why are there exceptions? Subshell energy levels can be very close. Sometimes moving one electron gives a more stable arrangement, especially when a d or f subshell is half-full or full.

Example: chromium is commonly written as [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ rather than [Ar] 3d⁴ 4s². This gives the 3d subshell a half-full arrangement. Copper similarly has a full 3d subshell.

5. Three useful rules for orbitals
  • Aufbau principle: fill lower-energy orbitals first.
  • Pauli exclusion principle: each orbital holds at most 2 electrons, with opposite spins.
  • Hund's rule: orbitals of equal energy get one electron each before electrons pair up.

Think of seats: use the lowest-energy seats first, two people maximum per orbital, and spread out across equal-energy seats before sharing.

6. Why do shell diagrams simplify things? A shell diagram groups electrons by shell, which is useful for seeing patterns. It does not show the individual s, p, d and f orbitals or all the details of their energies.

For introductory chemistry, shell totals are a handy map. For transition metals and heavier elements, orbital configurations explain the details more accurately.
